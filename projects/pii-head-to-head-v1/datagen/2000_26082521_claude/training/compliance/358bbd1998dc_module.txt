Restricted Information Categories: What County Health Department Staff Must Know
Annual Privacy & Security Refresher — Training Handout / Facilitator Script
Office of Compliance, Privacy Unit

I. Purpose of This Session
    A. Every employee, volunteer, student intern, and contractor of this department handles information that is restricted by law, contract, or department policy.
    B. This session covers three things:
        1. Which categories of information are restricted.
        2. What happens when restricted information is mishandled.
        3. How to recognize a mishandling risk before it becomes an incident.
    C. Facilitator note: allow 45 minutes. Sign-in sheet is submitted to the Training Coordinator within five business days.

II. Category One — Protected Health Information (PHI)
    A. Definition: health information created or received by this department that identifies an individual or could reasonably be used to identify one.
    B. Restricted whether it is spoken, written, faxed, photographed, or stored electronically.
    C. Fields that make a record identifiable include:
        1. Patient name; medical record number; health plan beneficiary number.
        2. Date of birth; date of service; date of death.
        3. Street address; ZIP code; telephone number; email address.
        4. Social Security number; driver's license number.
        5. Photographs and any other unique identifying characteristic.
    D. Common sources in our work: communicable disease case files, immunization records, WIC files, clinical encounter notes, laboratory results, contact tracing logs.
    E. Reminder: a case file remains PHI after the case is closed and after the individual is deceased.

III. Category Two — Communicable Disease and Public Health Investigation Records
    A. State law grants these records protection above and beyond the general health privacy rule.
    B. Includes: case investigation forms, contact tracing interview notes, outbreak line lists, exposure notification worksheets, laboratory reports of reportable conditions.
    C. These records may not be released to law enforcement, employers, schools, landlords, or the media absent a specific legal authority reviewed by the Records Custodian.
    D. Sub-category — heightened-sensitivity conditions:
        1. HIV/AIDS status and related test results.
        2. Sexually transmitted infection records.
        3. Tuberculosis case and contact records.
        4. Substance use disorder treatment records received from a federally assisted program.
        5. Disclosure of these requires written authorization specific to the condition. A general release form is not sufficient.

IV. Category Three — Personally Identifiable Information (PII) of Employees and Members of the Public
    A. Employee records: Social Security number, home address, personal telephone number, direct deposit account and routing numbers, benefit elections, medical accommodation files, disciplinary records.
    B. Public-facing program records: applicant income documentation, household composition, immigration status, benefit eligibility determinations.
    C. Vital records: birth and death certificate applications and supporting documentation.
    D. Payment information: cardholder name, primary account number, expiration date, security code. Staff may not write payment card data on paper under any circumstance.

V. Category Four — Restricted Business and Security Information
    A. Not personal, but still restricted:
        1. Draft policies, legal advice, and attorney-client communications.
        2. Procurement records prior to award; bid evaluations; unsigned contracts.
        3. Facility security plans, alarm codes, key inventories, badge access logs.
        4. Network diagrams, server names, system credentials, VPN configuration.
        5. Emergency preparedness plans marked "For Official Use Only."
    B. Personnel investigation files remain restricted while the investigation is open, regardless of who requests them.

VI. Consequences of Mishandling
    A. Consequences to the individual whose information was disclosed:
        1. Loss of employment, housing, or custody.
        2. Stigma, harassment, or domestic violence risk.
        3. Identity theft and financial loss.
        4. Loss of trust in the health department, which reduces participation in testing and case investigation and harms the whole community.
    B. Consequences to the employee:
        1. Verbal counseling and mandatory retraining for a first minor lapse.
        2. Written reprimand, suspension without pay, or termination for negligent or repeated disclosure.
        3. Referral to the licensing board where the employee holds a professional license.
        4. Criminal penalties under state and federal law for knowing disclosure or disclosure for personal gain, including fines and imprisonment.
        5. Personal civil liability. The department's indemnification does not extend to intentional acts outside the scope of employment.
    C. Consequences to the department:
        1. Mandatory breach notification to affected individuals, the state agency, and in larger events, the media.
        2. Civil monetary penalties and corrective action plans.
        3. Loss of data-sharing agreements with state registries and laboratory partners.
        4. Audit findings that reduce grant funding.
    D. Snooping is a violation on its own. Accessing a record you have no business need to access is a reportable incident even if you tell no one what you saw. All access to the electronic health record and the disease surveillance system is logged and reviewed.

VII. Scenario Quiz — Facilitator Reads Aloud
    A. Instructions: read each scenario, allow discussion, then read the answer.
    B. Scenario 1. A nurse recognizes a name on a case investigation line list as a neighbor. The nurse is not assigned to that investigation but opens the record to see how the neighbor is doing.
        1. Question: is this a violation? What should the nurse have done?
        2. Answer: yes. Curiosity is not a business need. The access is logged, and the nurse should have closed the list without opening the record and notified their supervisor of the potential conflict so the case could be reassigned.
    C. Scenario 2. A staff member takes a paper contact tracing worksheet home to finish calls in the evening. The worksheet is left in the car overnight, and the car is broken into.
        1. Question: what has occurred, and what is the reporting obligation?
        2. Answer: an unauthorized disclosure of restricted records. The staff member must report to the Privacy Officer the same day, no later than the next business day. Removing restricted records from the facility without written approval is itself a policy violation, separate from the theft.
    D. Scenario 3. A local reporter calls the front desk and asks how many people at a named workplace tested positive during an outbreak.
        1. Question: how does the front desk respond?
        2. Answer: the front desk does not confirm or deny that an investigation exists. All media inquiries are transferred to the Public Information Officer. Even aggregate numbers can identify individuals in a small workplace.
    E. Scenario 4. A staff member emails an outbreak line list to a colleague at a partner clinic to speed up follow-up. The list goes to the colleague's personal webmail address because the work address was bouncing.
        1. Question: what is wrong here?
        2. Answer: transmission of restricted information to an unsecured, non-department address. A data-sharing agreement and encrypted transmission are required. The correct step is to contact the Help Desk about the bounce, not to route around it.
    F. Scenario 5. A supervisor asks a payroll clerk to look up an employee's home address so a birthday card can be mailed.
        1. Question: may the clerk do this?
        2. Answer: no. Access to employee PII is limited to a payroll or benefits purpose. A supervisor's request does not create a business need. The clerk should decline and, if pressed, notify Human Resources.
    G. Scenario 6. During a video call from home, a staff member's household member walks behind the screen while a case file is displayed.
        1. Question: is this reportable?
        2. Answer: report it to the Privacy Officer for assessment. Telework requires a private workspace, a privacy screen or positioned monitor, and locking the screen when stepping away.

VIII. If You Think Something Went Wrong
    A. Report immediately. Do not attempt to retrieve, delete, or correct the disclosure on your own.
    B. Contact the Privacy Officer or the Compliance Hotline. Reports may be made anonymously.
    C. Retaliation against a person who reports in good faith is prohibited and is itself a disciplinary offense.
    D. Prompt self-reporting is a mitigating factor in every disciplinary review. Concealment is an aggravating factor.

IX. Acknowledgment
    A. Each attendee signs the Confidentiality Acknowledgment annually.
    B. The signed acknowledgment is filed with the Training Coordinator and retained for the duration of employment plus six years.
