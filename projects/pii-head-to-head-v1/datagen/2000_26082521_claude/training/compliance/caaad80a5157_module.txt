SLIDE SCRIPT — PRIVACY AND SAFEGUARDS MODULE
For delivery by the branch manager or the Privacy Officer at the monthly staff meeting. Read the narrative; the slides carry only the headings.

Slide 1, "What We Hold." Speak plainly here. Tell the room that in the ordinary course of a residential file this office takes custody of a party's Social Security Number, date of birth, driver's license number, bank routing and account numbers, loan number, payoff figures, and home address, and that we hold all of it at once, in one folder, which is exactly the combination someone would need to open credit in that person's name or redirect their closing funds. Point out that we did not ask for this responsibility as a separate business line; it arrives attached to every order we accept.

Slide 2, "The Two Rules That Prevent Most Incidents." First rule: information leaves this office only through a channel the file authorizes, which means a signed authorization, a written lender instruction, a legal demand, or return to the party who gave it to us. Second rule: a caller's knowledge of the file is not identification. Emphasize the second rule twice. Every diverted wire this industry has seen began with someone who knew the address, the closing date, and the names, because all of that is available from a listing or a courthouse index.

Slide 3, "Where It Actually Goes Wrong." Walk through the ordinary failures without naming anyone: a settlement statement faxed to a number that came off the incoming request rather than out of our directory; a file left open overnight in a room the cleaning contractor enters; a payoff account number read aloud to a helpful-sounding caller; a shared password so a coworker can cover the desk; a copy taken home over a weekend; a spreadsheet of pending orders forwarded out on the way to a new job. Ask the room how many of these they have seen a version of. The honest answer is usually most of them.

Slide 4, "What It Costs." Be specific rather than dramatic. To the customer: identity theft, a stolen wire, a fraudulent account, and months of unwinding. To the company: a complaint to the state insurance department, examination under the federal safeguards rule, indemnity demands from the underwriter, loss of lender approvals that drive our order count, and the expense of notifying everyone affected. To the employee: discipline up to and including termination, independent of any personal liability. State clearly that concealing an incident is treated as gravely as causing one, and that a good-faith report that turns out to be nothing carries no penalty whatsoever.

Slide 5, "Scenarios." Work these aloud with the room; do not distribute answers in advance. First, a caller who correctly recites the property address and closing time asks for the seller's payoff account number so they can call the lender directly. Second, a facsimile of a full settlement statement is confirmed as delivered to a brokerage number found nowhere in the file. Third, an electronic mail message quoting earlier correspondence correctly arrives from an apparent lender contact with revised wire instructions naming a different receiving bank. Fourth, a departing employee asks to retain a spreadsheet of open orders containing borrower names, addresses, telephone numbers, and loan numbers. For each, ask three questions: what class of information is at issue, what verification or authorization was required, and who must be told and by when.

Slide 6, "Close." One password per person. Nothing in the recycling bin. Nothing home over the weekend. Verify wire changes by outbound call to a number from our own records, never the number on the incoming document. Same-day reporting to the branch manager and the Privacy Officer. Then take questions and record attendance on the training log.
